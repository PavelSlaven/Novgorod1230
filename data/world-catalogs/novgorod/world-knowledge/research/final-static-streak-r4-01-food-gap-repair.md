# Final static streak R4.01: churning and damaged egg shells

Status: research note only. These are modern, general food-science and food
safety observations, not claims about a particular Novgorod household, a
medieval food rule, a present food item, or its safe condition. They support
conditional reconstruction only; no exact recipe, duration, temperature,
diagnosis, safety promise, or outcome is licensed by this note.

## Cream, work, temperature, and butter separation

Source: [University of Wisconsin Center for Dairy Research, *Butter Science
101*, sections "Tempering & Crystallization", "Churning", and "Working,
Salting, Packaging, & Chilling"](https://cdr.wisc.edu/butter-science-101).

- Butter-making is a physical separation process: churning transforms cream
  from an oil-in-water emulsion to butter, a water-in-oil emulsion. It causes
  fat globules to coalesce into solid butter granules/clumps while buttermilk
  is released and drained.
- Churning is work on a fat-in-water system, not a guaranteed instantaneous
  conversion. The Center identifies tempering and the balance of crystallized
  and liquid milkfat as material to churning and final texture; it also notes
  that cream temperature changes during crystallization. Therefore a
  reconstruction may treat cream warmth/coolness and the amount of agitation
  as materially relevant to how readily grains form and to the character of
  the resulting butter; it must not infer an exact threshold or a certain yield
  from either fact alone.

**Gameplay/reconstruction boundary.** When an actor has actual cream and a
means to agitate it, narration may describe repeated working, gradual clumping
or visible separation as conditional physical observations. It may also say
that unusually cold or warm cream can call for different handling or produce a
less convenient result. The system must not conjure cream, butter, a churn,
milk supply, a fixed quantity, an exact traditional method, or a successful
batch merely because the actor asks to make butter.

## Cracked shell, warmth, and contamination risk

Source: [USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service, *Shell Eggs from Farm to
Table*, sections "How Does Salmonella Contaminate Eggs?", "Why Should Eggs Be
Refrigerated?", "Is It Safe to Use Eggs that Have Cracks?", and "Safe Storage
in Stores"](https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/eggs/shell-eggs-farm-table).

- A shell egg can carry bacteria in its contents or on its shell; FSIS notes
  that bacteria can enter through shell pores. A crack is therefore a material
  loss of the intact shell barrier, not merely a cosmetic defect. FSIS directs
  consumers not to buy cracked eggs.
- FSIS states that bacteria on or in eggs multiply at room temperature and
  that refrigeration reduces Salmonella growth. A warm, cracked, or heavily
  handled egg thus calls for more caution than a clean intact cold-stored egg;
  those visible conditions alone do not establish whether this particular egg
  is contaminated or unsafe.

**Gameplay/reconstruction boundary.** A visible crack can support a response
such as "the protective shell is compromised" and a cautious handling choice;
warm storage or warm handling can increase the food-safety concern. It cannot
prove infection, spoilage, illness, a safe-use window, a legally required
disposition in 1230, or a specific outcome after eating. Historical household
practice, local availability, exact storage conditions, and the resulting
state remain separate, committed facts rather than deductions from this
general evidence.
