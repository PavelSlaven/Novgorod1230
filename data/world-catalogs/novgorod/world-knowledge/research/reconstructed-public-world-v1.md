# Reconstructed public world v1

**Status:** editorial rationale for the paired authoring fragment; not an historical source, legal code, medical guide, combat system, or runtime activation.

## Scope and method

This reconstruction closes four static World Knowledge gaps identified under WK §§0.2–0.3: public consequences of armed participation, ordinary dispute escalation, speech/naming/register, and practical support around illness or impairment. It uses only qualitative, game-useful premises for Novgorod Land in 1200–1300. Every claim is marked `editorial`, `unknown`, and `medium`; it is a reviewed-plausibility candidate, never an attestation.

Existing pack premises already establish bounded anchors: a 1220 armed court and five regiments, a hunting bow and helmet find, household provisioning and transport; dispute letters, property/witness comparators, public reputation and kin support; Old-Russian/dialect documentary material and bounded name forms; and physiology of injury, fever, wound healing, activity, support, and disability. The new fragment composes these anchors. It neither overwrites their limits nor creates people, property, institutions, equipment, illness, authority, or outcomes.

## Warfare and armed groups

An armed group is not a uniform army inventory. Personal means, role, access, movement on foot or with an animal, and upkeep make a plausible difference to what someone carries and can do. A shield is held for cover, armour covers only particular body areas, and a weapon needs access and reach; none of these is an instruction to make, use, or rate it. Mounted participation needs an available horse, fodder, care, and harness, whereas a foot participant carries personal equipment. Senior coordination and messenger travel are ordinary possible arrangements only after people, destination, and access exist.

Food, fuel, carrying capacity, attention, delay, injury, absence, and lost equipment connect an established armed episode to household life. An injured person may need support, shelter, or slower carriage; this is not a medical rule. Bystanders can seek distance, allies, facts, or restraint around known arms. No claim supplies tactics, force size, command hierarchy, a campaign, a current weapon, or a combat result.

## Disputes, public standing, and escalation

An ordinary conflict begins with accounts that may disagree. The owner, current holder, witness, messenger, kin, and local authority are possible roles only if already materialized. Asking questions, recalling an account, negotiating, offering return or apology, seeking kin, and asking a recognized authority for hearing, mediation, protection, or referral are possible paths—not mandatory stages and not executable law.

Public handling can broaden both support and scrutiny. Standing, relationships, reputation, and witnesses affect access and risk, but do not decide truth, guilt, tariff, jurisdiction, or judgment. A witness is bounded by perception, interest, memory, and what they were told.

## Speech, naming, and register

Ordinary speech changes with relation, age, role, audience, and purpose. Familiar talk need not sound like church or official address; short requests, repetitions, clarifications, and assessments can stand beside established formulas. A personal name can be disambiguated with a father-name, spouse-derived form, byname, occupation, kin connection, or place, but no form proves status, marriage, office, ancestry, or legal identity.

The target is legible historical flavour, not a fixed name generator, pseudo-archaic dialect engine, or licence for contemporary slang. Unclear words, names, accents, and messages can prompt repetition, explanation, or a known intermediary.

## Illness, impairment, and support

Illness, injury, fatigue, and impairment may rearrange work, pace, routes, tools, help, and rest. When safe and available, warmth, food, drink, rest, company, and a changed task are practical support, not a treatment protocol or evidence of efficacy. Support is constrained by people, time, shelter, resources, weather, trust, and work pressure.

Responses remain humanly variable: help, respect, impatience, avoidance, doubt, or exclusion are possible. Neither pity nor stigma is automatic. Capacity can differ by task, day, environment, fatigue, and available help; one observed limitation does not define a person or determine a result.
