# Oily or fatty surface film on dry skin: candidate source basis

## Scope

One candidate physical premise only. It distinguishes an ordinary oily or fatty application from a medical emollient claim: only an application that actually remains as a continuous hydrophobic surface film is in scope. The fragment reuses the already compiled concept `wk:biology_physiology:skin-emollient-and-occlusive-film`; it creates neither a recipe nor a new treatment pathway.

## Candidate premise

An oily or fatty skin application that actually remains as a continuous hydrophobic surface film can reduce evaporative water loss from the skin surface. It can therefore inform an ordinary attempt to coat dry skin, but does not prove that a particular animal fat or oil will form such a film, suit a person, remain in place, soften a particular crack, or heal injury.

## Source and actual access

- DermNet, [*Emollients and moisturisers*](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/emollients-and-moisturisers), by Libby Whittaker and dermatologist Amanda Oakley, accessed through official-page web fallback after local `browser-harness --doctor` found no live daemon. Read “Emollients” and “Occlusives”: oily substances are listed in emollients; an occlusive agent provides a lipophilic oil layer on the skin surface that reduces evaporative water loss. The page names clinical product ingredients and formulations, not a universal efficacy result for arbitrary ordinary fat.

## Limits

No medical treatment, historical practice, dose, recipe, lesion outcome, burn first aid, antimicrobial action, infection management, or assertion that every fat, oil, wax, or coating is safe/effective is proposed. In particular, this claim does not license grease for burns, weeping skin, or infected skin.
