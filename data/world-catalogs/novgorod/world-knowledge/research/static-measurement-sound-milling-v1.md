## NIST — Weighing and Scales FAQs

[Source](https://www.nist.gov/pml/owm/faqs/weighing-and-scales-faqs), Mathematical agreement in determining net weight.

- Gross load includes contents and packaging tare. Comparing contents requires accounting for tare separately; a heavier sack need not contain more grain.

Limits: Gross load comprises packaging tare and net product; use the physical relation, not current US law or digital-scale requirements.

## Canadian Grain Commission — Test weight calculators

[Source](https://www.grainscanada.gc.ca/en/grain-quality/grain-grading/grading-factors/test-weight-calculator.html), Determining grain weights and volumes; Unit equivalents.

- Grain mass and volume are linked by bulk density: equal volumes of batches with different bulk densities have different masses. Conversion needs an applicable density, not only the grain name.
- Comparing measures requires identifying the quantity and unit: mass cannot be equated directly with volume, and the same measure name need not denote the same volume.

Limits: Mass-volume conversion uses grain test weight and identified units; no medieval measure or fixed batch density is asserted.

## FAO — Small mills in Africa

[Source](https://www.fao.org/4/j8482e/j8482e.pdf), Printed pp. 5–7 (PDF pp. 13–15), milling mechanisms and plate mills.

- In milling, grain fractures under compression and shear at contacts, producing a range of particle sizes including fines and dust. Load, contact and grain condition affect the result.
- Dry grain is more brittle during milling; moist grain can deform more before fracture. The same mechanical action therefore need not give the same product at different moisture contents.
- In the described plate milling, narrowing the working gap raises pressure on grain and changes fineness; contact between the working surfaces themselves causes excess wear. This is a qualitative mechanism, not a setting for every mill.

Limits: Compression/shear fracture, moisture-dependent deformation and plate-gap relation. Modern equipment examples do not establish historical availability.

## OpenStax — University Physics Volume 1, 17.3 Sound Intensity

[Source](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/17-3-sound-intensity), Opening examples; spherical spreading.

- In a free field, a spreading sound wave distributes energy over increasing area, so intensity decreases with distance. Reflections, obstacles and the medium can change actual audibility.
- Strong surrounding noise can make speech difficult to understand even near the speaker. Proximity does not guarantee intelligibility when other sounds mask speech.

Limits: Sound spreading and masking by surrounding noise, without a local audible distance or guarantee.

## OpenStax — Introduction to Behavioral Neuroscience, 7.1 Acoustic Cues and Signals

[Source](https://openstax.org/books/introduction-behavioral-neuroscience/pages/7-1-acoustic-cues-and-signals), Room reflections and perceptible delay.

- Reflected sound travels a longer path and can arrive after direct sound; indoors this produces delayed reflections that alter the heard sequence of sounds.

Limits: Longer reflected paths can arrive after direct sound; no exact church acoustics or singing custom.

## FHWA — Noise Barrier Design Handbook, 3 Acoustical Considerations

[Source](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/Environment/noise/noise_barriers/design_construction/design/design03.cfm), 3.3.3 Meteorological effects.

- Wind and temperature gradients refract airborne sound: under some conditions downwind propagation bends toward the ground and upwind propagation away from it. Audibility depends on propagation conditions, not only voice level.

Limits: Wind/temperature gradients refract sound; conditional outdoor propagation, not local audibility or modern traffic.

## OpenStax — Introductory Statistics 2e, 3.2 Independent and Mutually Exclusive Events

[Source](https://openstax.org/books/introductory-statistics-2e/pages/3-2-independent-and-mutually-exclusive-events), Independent Events.

- For genuinely independent trials, a previous outcome does not change the next outcome's probability. In an independent fair-roll model repetitions are possible and do not make a reversal obligatory; this does not establish fairness of a physical die.

Limits: Independence and fair-die model; no attestation of a physical die, historical game or current fairness.
