## 1. Cloven-hoof traces: cautious candidate premises

### Supported general premises

- A hoof trace should be interpreted from several observable features, not from
  one outline: University of Arizona Extension lists digit count, claw marks,
  track length/width, and track-path pattern as useful identification features.
  Its cattle plate describes a two-digit cloven hoof, a blocky print with round
  tips, and different front/hind reference dimensions. This supports only a
  conditional comparison of several trace features. It does not establish a
  species identity from them.
- Alaska Department of Fish and Game describes a moose print as two
  crescent-shaped halves with two dew claws; tips can register more deeply in
  snow or soft ground. North Dakota Game and Fish gives broad adult moose
  front/rear length, width, and walking-stride ranges. These are reference
  features, not an identification key or a local observation.
- NMSU Extension explicitly warns that a print made by the same animal can look
  different in mud, dust, or snow; sun can enlarge and distort snow tracks; age,
  sex, and individual variation change track size. University of Arizona also
  says environmental setting, soil condition, nutrition, and actual animal size
  alter track/scat appearance, and calls its representations broad references.
  Therefore size, split-hoof outline, dewclaw registration, and stride are
  evidence to weigh together, not deterministic taxon markers.

### Bounded use

An evidence-grounded narration may say a clear large split-hoof trail is
*compatible with* multiple ungulate possibilities and describe observed shape,
dimensions, substrate, dewclaw marks, trackway pattern, and corroborating sign
when actually present. It must retain uncertainty where features overlap or the
imprint is degraded. A single print, absent corroboration, does not establish
moose, cattle, individual identity, number, sex, age, direction, freshness,
local presence, route, or encounter.

## 2. Equine digestion and abdominal-pain context: cautious candidate premises

### Supported general premises

- Rutgers NJAES states that horses are hindgut fermenters: microbes in the cecum
  and large colon ferment fibre and other material escaping foregut digestion.
  This is a qualitative physiological relation, not a ration, intake quantity,
  individual digestive state, or health outcome.
- University of Pennsylvania School of Veterinary Medicine says changes in hay
  or concentrate, or sudden pasture access, can trigger colic while gut microbes
  adjust; it recommends gradual changes. University of Illinois College of
  Veterinary Medicine likewise says a sudden winter-hay-to-lush-pasture change
  may alter the gut microbiome and may cause colic. Both support risk/context,
  not inevitability or a specific cause in a given horse.
- UF/IFAS College of Veterinary Medicine defines equine colic as abdominal pain
  or discomfort and lists possible signs including reduced appetite, lethargy,
  repeated lying/rolling, flank watching, kicking at the abdomen, and reduced
  fecal output. Illinois lists similar pain, gastrointestinal, and general signs
  and says to call a veterinarian immediately when signs are noticed.
- A distended abdomen is one possible sign in the Illinois list, while its
  clinical work-up uses examination, rectal palpation, ultrasound, and other
  findings to determine cause. Thus visible distension is **not** a diagnosis of
  bloat, gas colic, obstruction, or any specific condition.

### Bounded use

These sources support an ordinary safety context: abdominal-pain signs are a
reason to stop normal exertion/avoid forcing the horse and seek an experienced
veterinarian or equivalent qualified animal-care help. The direct sources
support only safe brief walking after veterinary contact and explicitly reject
continuous/exhausting walking; they do not supply a universal "stop work"
protocol. Do not narrate treatment, medication, feeding/withholding protocol,
dose, prognosis, cause, severity, recovery, or diagnosis from a single sign.

### Sources read

1. Rutgers New Jersey Agricultural Experiment Station, [*The Equine Hindgut
   Microbiome*](https://njaes.rutgers.edu/e375/), accessed 2026-09-05.
   Directly read: hindgut-fermenter classification, cecum/large-colon microbial
   fermentation, and diet as an influence on microbial-community structure.
2. University of Pennsylvania School of Veterinary Medicine, [*Colic — What You
   Can Do*](https://www.vet.upenn.edu/new-bolton-center/clinical-services/emergency-critical-care/colic-what-you-can-do/),
   accessed 2026-09-05. Directly read: dietary-change risk, gradual transition,
   veterinary escalation, and non-exhausting-walk limit.
3. University of Illinois College of Veterinary Medicine, [*Spring
   Pasture-Induced Colic in Horses*](https://vetmed.illinois.edu/pet-health-columns/spring-pasture-induced-colic-in-horses/),
   accessed 2026-09-05. Directly read: rapid-fermentation/diet-change context,
   listed signs, immediate veterinarian contact, and diagnostic uncertainty.
4. University of Florida IFAS / College of Veterinary Medicine, [*First Aid for
   Horse Owners*](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/VM275), accessed
   2026-09-05. Directly read: colic as abdominal pain/discomfort, sign list,
   and cause-dependent veterinary-intervention variability.

### What remains unknown

No reviewed source supports a universal historical husbandry practice, a
particular horse's feed, microbiome, pain, cause, bloat diagnosis, obstruction,
or outcome. The clinical sources are modern veterinary guidance, not 1230
treatment authority; this note must not become a treatment procedure or dose
source.
