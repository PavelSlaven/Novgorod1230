## Per-claim verdicts

### Tree leaf/flower sequence — REJECT

Candidate claims interspecies variation, a bounded qualitative proposition, but
the cited exact source is unavailable and its metadata do not identify the
fallback Gougherty article. The fallback record is *Sequence of flower and leaf
emergence in deciduous trees is linked to ecological traits, phylogenetics, and
climate*, *New Phytologist* 220, PMID 29900552; candidate names a different
title, *American Journal of Botany* 105(6), “et al.” authors, and PMID
29629965. Candidate evidence cannot be checked at its declared anchor.
Reject exact payload; replacement requires corrected immutable source metadata
and a re-review.

### Tree leaf/flower sequence — APPROVE after repair

The repaired candidate cites the exact PubMed record: Andrew V. Gougherty and
Steven W. Gougherty, *Sequence of flower and leaf emergence in deciduous trees
is linked to ecological traits, phylogenetics, and climate*, *New Phytologist*
220(1):121–131 (2018), DOI `10.1111/nph.15270`, PMID 29900552. Its `Abstract`
describes patterns of flower-and-leaf-emergence sequence in deciduous tree
species and reports phylogenetic and climate-related patterns. The repaired
modal sequence claim is within that support. It remains no calendar, taxon,
weather, current tree state, fruiting, yield, or scene fact.

### Nettle bast fibre — APPROVE

The reopened PMC article’s retting/decortication discussion states that retting
uses microorganisms and moisture to remove much cellular tissue and pectins,
and describes subsequent decortication/separation of bast fibre bundles. This
supports only the candidate’s modern `Urtica dioica` process relation.
It does not establish historical Novgorod use, an available plant, a season,
yield, tool, duration, or a successful output. This is scholarship/review
evidence, not proof of an individual game scene.

### Flood woody debris — NEEDS_REVIEW

The USGS-hosted paper is a primary field study of the ephemeral Kuiseb River in
southwestern Africa after one measured flood. It directly reports transport and
retention there, and tests downstream hydrologic decay and in-channel tree
growth. The candidate lifts this into universal context scope for a generic
river flood and generic local hydrologic/channel features. Its medium/inferred
qualifier and “can” wording reduce overclaiming, but one ephemeral-river study
does not independently establish that cross-region generalization. Retain no
approval until a broader primary synthesis/second river system supports it, or
narrow the claim to the observed system.

### Flood woody debris — APPROVE after reconciliation

The USGS abstract directly reports flood transport and retention of marked
wood, and reports effects of downstream hydrologic decay and in-channel trees
on retention. Its concluding sentence identifies retentive obstacles and
associated organic debris as a feature common to fluvial ecosystems regardless
of hydrologic regime. The candidate is modal and inferred: it says a flood
*can* transport woody debris and local hydrologic/channel features *can*
affect retention. That conditional mechanism is supported; it does not assert
that every flood, river, feature, or scene has debris or a particular result.
No local debris, access, quantity, safety, ownership, or outcome follows.

### Poor circulation and dissolved oxygen — APPROVE

The reopened USGS Water Science School paragraph explicitly lists poor flushing
or circulation as a factor that can result in low dissolved oxygen. Candidate
preserves “can contribute,” contains no oxygen value or fish claim, and does
not create a waterbody state. The USGS page is a general official explanation,
not a quantified oxygen or mortality model.

### Rain after dry spell and dissolved oxygen — APPROVE

The same reopened USGS paragraph explicitly lists sudden rain after a dry spell
as a factor that can result in low dissolved oxygen. Candidate preserves the
qualified relation and excludes amount, runoff chemistry, local waterbody,
pollution, fish, and outcome. It must not be combined with other premises to
assert low oxygen without actual water-state evidence.

## Cross-cutting limits

- A single claim never establishes all signs, a disease, a cause, or a
  guaranteed result.
- The two USGS dissolved-oxygen statements are possible contributors, not
  causal sufficiency or a water-quality calculation.
- The woody-debris evidence is geographically and hydrologically narrow.
- The tree candidate fails source identity before its scope can be accepted.
