# Final static streak R2.01: reusable gap premises

Status: research note only. Modern general evidence, not a claim about Novgorod in 1230. It supplies conditional, observable world premises; it supplies no historical construction, recipe, quantities, prices, deterministic outcome, diagnosis, or recovery guarantee.

## 1. Stored root vegetables: freezing versus decay

Source: UC Davis Postharvest Research and Extension Center, ["Potato (Early Crop)"](https://postharvest.ucdavis.edu/produce-facts-sheets/potato), **Freezing Injury** and **Pathological Disorders**.

- Freezing injury can present as water-soaked or glassy tissue, then tissue breakdown after thawing.
- The same storage domain also has bacterial and fungal rots; handling damage can lead to water loss, shriveling, and decay.
- Game premise: visible wet/glassy, softened, collapsed, discolored, or decaying pieces can justify inspection and sorting from apparently sound stock. Appearance alone does not identify agent, prove safety of a neighboring piece, or restore damaged food. Sorting limits loss exposure; it cannot guarantee that a stored batch is sound.

## 2. Powdery surface on leaves: mildew is a hypothesis, not proof

Sources: Cornell University, ["Powdery Mildew"](https://greenhouse.cornell.edu/pests-diseases/disease-factsheets/powdery-mildew/); North Carolina State Extension, ["Powdery Mildew of Strawberry"](https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/powdery-mildew-of-strawberry), **Diagnostic Procedures**.

- Typical powdery mildew is a whitish fungal growth on leaf, stem, or petal surfaces; sparse growth may require strong light, a hand lens, or microscopy to detect.
- White surface signs can be confused with another residue; N.C. State gives whitefly wax as one specific example and says magnification distinguishes fungal structures.
- Game premise: a white/gray powdery coating, patch, or felt-like mat supports a field suspicion and careful inspection. It does not prove mildew, species, spread, cause, or remedy. Wiping/removing loose surface material changes what is visible, not necessarily whether underlying infection exists.

## 3. Stored ice: heat gain and melt remain conditional

Source: U.S. National Park Service, ["Ice House & Tack Room"](https://home.nps.gov/places/ice-house-tack-room.htm).

- The documented icehouse used insulating material around and between stored blocks; its floor directed meltwater to an opening rather than leaving it against the structure.
- Even an insulated store melted ice, though slowly; opening, insulation condition, separation/packing, and meltwater drainage are therefore relevant observable state, not flavor text.
- Game premise: dry insulating packing or gaps between blocks, a protected enclosure, and a functioning melt path can reduce expected heat gain or water damage. They cannot stop melting, promise a duration, or establish any particular historical icehouse design.

## 4. Hot ash-lye and soap attempt: hazardous and conditional

Sources: Alabama Cooperative Extension, ["Wood Ashes For Lime And Potash"](https://www.aces.edu/blog/topics/lawn-garden/wood-ashes-for-lime-and-potash/); CDC/ATSDR, ["Sodium Hydroxide: Medical Management Guidelines"](https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/MMG/MMGDetails.aspx?mmgid=246&toxid=45); Open University, ["DIY: Make Your Own Soap"](https://www.open.edu/openlearn/science-maths-technology/science/diy-make-your-own-soap).

- Wood ash retains alkaline metal compounds; Alabama Extension notes its historical use as a lye source, but gives no basis for treating a particular ash liquor as known-strength reagent.
- CDC describes lye (sodium hydroxide) and its solutions as corrosive, with severe skin and especially eye hazard; contact, splashes, hot liquid, and mist are meaningful hazards. This is a hazard analogy, not a claim that ash liquor has a measured sodium-hydroxide concentration.
- Open University's ash-derived dilute potassium-hydroxide attempt with oil failed, while later purification and changed conditions produced soap. A separated, grainy, oily, watery, or otherwise unsatisfactory mass may therefore be a failed or incomplete attempt, not a successful product.
- Game premise: an actor may attempt controlled mixing/heating of alkaline liquor and fat, with a conditional result. Unknown-strength liquor, uncertain inputs, poor separation, splashing, or unsafe heat can yield a failure, damaged batch, or injury risk. Do not infer a recipe, safe recovery procedure, usable soap, or guaranteed emulsion from the attempt.

## 5. Manure or compost heap: self-heating can become fire risk

Source: U.S. National Park Service, ["Fire Prevention 52: Spontaneous Combustion—Fact or Fiction?"](https://www.nps.gov/articles/fire-prevention-52-spontaneous-combustion.htm); Cornell Waste Management Institute, ["Health and Safety Precautions"](https://compost.css.cornell.edu/Factsheets/FS9.html).

- Decomposing manure, compost, mulch, and leaf piles can self-heat through biological, chemical, or physical processes; large piles can retain more heat.
- NPS specifically warns that smoke from a hay, mulch, leaf, manure, or compost pile is a fire emergency. It also cautions that spreading a smoking pile introduces oxygen and can trigger immediate fire.
- Game premise: warmth, steam/smoke, smell, pile size, dryness, and nearby dry straw/wood are relevant observations. A warm heap is not automatically aflame; a smoking heap is a dangerous state. Moving, opening, or aerating it is conditional risk management, not a guaranteed harmless fix.

## 6. Thick porridge or starch food: bottom scorching is local

Sources: University of Kentucky Cooperative Extension, ["Cooking with Heat"](https://fcs.mgcafe.uky.edu/sites/fcs.mgcafe.uky.edu/files/fn-ssb_002_cookingwithheat_2022.pdf); Clemson Cooperative Extension, ["Basics of Jelly Making"](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/basics-of-jelly-making/); Georgia State Department of Education, ["Instructor's manual foundation course: school lunch"](https://dlg.usg.edu/record/dlg_ggpd_s-ga-be300-pa2-bm1-b1969-bl8).

- Heated starch absorbs water, swells, and thickens; unsuitable heat or mixing can interrupt normal gelatinization.
- The Georgia manual states that material at bottom and sides cooks faster than the center as a starch mixture thickens, so low heat and stirring are needed. Clemson likewise directs frequent stirring as fruit mixtures thicken to prevent sticking and scorching.
- Game premise: a thickening porridge can scorch first at pan-contact surfaces while the rest remains less cooked. Lowering heat, stirring/scraping, and attention mitigate this bounded risk; they cannot unburn an already scorched portion, guarantee uniform cooking, or turn poor ingredients into a good dish.

## 7. Onion and plant water stress: inspect soil, do not promise recovery

Sources: University of Georgia Cooperative Extension, ["Onion Production Guide"](https://extension.uga.edu/publications/detail.html?number=B1198&title=onion-production-guide); UConn Extension, ["Helping Vegetable Crops Through Drought and Heat"](https://extension.uconn.edu/publication/vegetable-crops-drought-and-heat/); Iowa State University Extension, ["Why is my houseplant wilting?"](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/faq/why-my-houseplant-wilting).

- Onions are shallow-rooted and water stress reduces bulb size/yield, but Georgia notes that onions often do not visibly wilt under moisture stress. Visible leaf posture is therefore not a sufficient onion-water test.
- UConn recommends checking moisture below the surface in the active root zone rather than judging the surface alone. Its drought guidance identifies onion transplanting and bulb enlargement as particularly water-sensitive stages.
- Wilting in plants is ambiguous: Iowa State notes that both dry soil and over-wet, root-rotting conditions can prevent roots supplying leaves with water.
- Game premise: prioritize visibly dry beds after checking root-zone moisture and stage, especially when water is limited. Wilt, pale/drooping foliage, dry upper soil, and uneven bed condition justify investigation, not a certain diagnosis. Watering may relieve a genuinely dry, still-viable plant; it does not guarantee recovery or exclude disease, root damage, or excess-water stress.
