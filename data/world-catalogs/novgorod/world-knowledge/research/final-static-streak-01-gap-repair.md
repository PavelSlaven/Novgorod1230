## 1. Wet leather footwear or object

- The Canadian Conservation Institute says water damage varies with the
  leather's processing and condition; wet semi-tanned or vegetable-tanned
  leather may shrink or stiffen on drying, and heat-induced drying can cause
  desiccation, distortion, and splits. [CCI: Caring for leather, skin and
  fur](https://www.canada.ca/en/conservation-institute/services/preventive-conservation/guidelines-collections/caring-leather-skin-fur.html)
- The Smithsonian's wet-collections guide says wet leather can deform and dry
  stiff; it prescribes support/blocking to hold its shape, air circulation,
  and air drying. [Smithsonian NCP: *Quick Reference Guide: Salvaging Wet
  Collections Items* (PDF)](https://ncp.si.edu/sites/default/files/ncp/files/PRICE/Water/QUICK%20REFERENCE%20GUIDE%20Salvaging%20Wet%20Collections%20Items_PRICE_2020%20.pdf)

**Game-ready bounded claim.** While damp, leather may permit cautious
reshaping or support to reduce further distortion; let it dry slowly with the
support and away from strong heat. Wetting and drying can instead leave it
shrunken, stiff, stained, distorted, or split. Do not promise restoration of
the former fit, strength, or appearance; leather type and prior condition
matter.

## 2. Slaked lime and fresh lime mortar

- NIOSH identifies calcium hydroxide (hydrated/slaked lime) as causing eye,
  skin, and upper-respiratory irritation, including eye and skin burns; it
  directs prevention of skin/eye contact and washing contaminated skin.
  [CDC/NIOSH: *Calcium hydroxide*](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/npg/npgd0092.html)
- Northern Ireland's official stone-and-brick repointing guidance requires
  protection of new lime work for at least seven days and warns that lime work
  is vulnerable to frost for a long period. [Department for Communities:
  *Technical Note: Repointing Stone and Brick*](https://www.communities-ni.gov.uk/articles/technical-note-repointing-stone-and-brick)
- Cornwall Council specifies protection of fresh lime pointing from frost,
  rapid drying by wind/sun, and rain. [Cornwall Council: historic-building
  advice](https://www.cornwall.gov.uk/planning-and-building-control/conservation-and-planning/advice-for-works-to-historic-buildings/)

**Game-ready bounded claim.** Wet lime or fresh lime mortar is an eye/skin
hazard: avoid bare-skin and eye contact; modern contamination guidance is
copious water rinsing, not a period treatment. Cold, rain, saturation, rapid
drying, and freezing do not improve fresh mortar; they can interrupt or
weaken its setting/curing. Its result remains contingent on mix, exposure,
protection, and elapsed time—never a guaranteed sound bond or cure.

## 3. Deeply embedded splinter

- MedlinePlus directs washing hands and the area, describes home removal for
  an accessible splinter, and advises medical help for a deeply embedded
  splinter, infection signs, or a splinter in/near the eye.
  [NIH/MedlinePlus: *Splinter removal*](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/article/002137.htm)
- Mayo Clinic says not to try to remove an object deeply embedded in skin or
  muscle, and to obtain prompt care for a deeply embedded or painful object
  and for eye injuries. [Mayo Clinic: *Puncture wounds: First aid*](https://www.mayoclinic.org/first-aid/first-aid/basics/art-20056604)
- CDC's wound-management guidance treats vaccination history and wound type
  as relevant to tetanus prevention, rather than using antibiotics to prevent
  tetanus. [CDC: *Clinical Guidance for Wound Management to Prevent
  Tetanus*](https://www.cdc.gov/tetanus/hcp/clinical-guidance/index.html)

**Game-ready bounded claim.** Do not dig blindly. Assess whether the end is
visible, safely graspable, and whether the wound is deep or contaminated;
clean first. Attempt removal only for a visible end that can be safely
grasped. A deep or difficult splinter, one in/near an eye, or marked
inflammation calls for skilled help. Deep/dirty wounds and uncertain tetanus
protection are escalation boundaries, not a diagnosis or an automatic
infection outcome.
