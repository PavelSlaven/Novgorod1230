## Candidate premises (3–5)

1. **ANALOGY, medium — beaver cut sign.** A cut stump with a noticeably
   tapered/conical top and visible paired or diagonal incisor grooves is a
   beaver-compatible gnawing sign; a smooth saw or axe surface is a different
   mechanism. The US Forest Service damage key lists a conical stump with
   prominent tooth marks for beaver, and its guide distinguishes beaver's
   diagonal tooth marks from other damage. [USFS, *Animal Damage Management Handbook*](https://www.fs.usda.gov/pnw/pubs/pnw_gtr332.pdf)

2. **ANALOGY, medium — use several signs, not one.** Near water, chewed
   stumps/branches together with chips, a stick-and-mud dam or lodge, or
   webbed tracks make a beaver explanation more plausible than one damaged
   sapling alone. [National Park Service, *Beavers*](https://home.nps.gov/kaww/learn/nature/beavers.htm)
   describes those signs as a group. This is a transfer from *Castor
   canadensis* guidance to already-covered *Castor fiber* behaviour; it is not
   species identification, proof that a local beaver is present, or proof that
   a human did not also cut wood.

3. **FACT, medium — layered dark cloud as a near-term caution cue.** NOAA
   describes gray altostratus and nimbostratus as cloud types that usually
   precede continuous rain or snow. A traveller may treat their appearance as
   reason to prepare for wet conditions, not as certainty that rain will reach
   a particular path or at a particular time. [NOAA NESDIS, *What Are the
   Different Types of Clouds?*](https://www.nesdis.noaa.gov/about/k-12-education/atmosphere/what-are-the-different-types-of-clouds)

4. **FACT, medium — a sequence is stronger than one cloud.** The National
   Weather Service *National Weather Service Observing Handbook No. 1* describes a typical advancing-storm
   progression from cirrus through cirrostratus and darker altostratus to
   nimbostratus, after which rain begins and wind speed should increase. This
   supports watching a developing sequence and strengthening wind as a
   qualitative warning, not a forecast rule for every cloud bank, season,
   terrain, route, or rain amount. [NWS, *Federal Meteorological Handbook
   No. 1*](https://www.weather.gov/media/owlie/ObservingHandbook1_2010_508_compliant.pdf)
